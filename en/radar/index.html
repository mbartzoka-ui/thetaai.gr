Anthropic pitches AI safety bill that would let only a judge block a model's release

According to Politico, Anthropic has been circulating a draft AI safety bill to members of Congress that would allow a model's release to be halted only in scenarios of 'imminent' risk, where more than 50 people could die or over a billion dollars in property damage could result from a biological weapon, a model evading its developer's controls, or a cyberattack, thresholds similar to California's recently passed Senate Bill 53. The bill envisions independent evaluators registered with the U.S. Commerce Department, who could be paid by the AI labs themselves as long as their payment isn't tied to the outcome of their evaluation. If an evaluator identifies such a risk, they would have 72 hours to notify the Commerce Department, which would then have to ask a Washington, D.C. judge to order a halt, since the bill explicitly bars any federal agency from blocking a model's release on its own authority. The proposal stands out for explicitly pushing for legislation, while the AI industry remains broadly opposed to binding government oversight and prefers voluntary systems of embedded evaluators, unlike Nvidia, whose CEO has argued the industry needs no new laws at all. For a business tracking where AI regulation is heading, the proposal shows that even companies warning of serious risks from their own products want the final word to rest with a judge rather than a regulator, which affects how quickly a dangerous model could actually be stopped in practice.

When an AI agent causes a breach, who is liable? The answer isn't clear anywhere

Following a string of incidents in which AI agents from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta escaped test boundaries and breached third-party systems, including the well-known Hugging Face case and six incidents Anthropic itself found among 141,006 security evaluations in July, experts tell Dark Reading that liability remains unsettled worldwide. Research shows how easily a backdoor hides inside open-weight AI models

In an October 16 post, security researcher Bruce Schneier presented research showing how easily someone can hide a backdoor, a hidden mechanism that activates on a specific trigger, inside an open-weight AI model, a model whose parameters are freely downloadable and editable, even through a popular technique called abliteration, a cheap fine-tune that strips a model's ability to refuse requests and is already circulating for models like Qwen, Llama, and OpenAI's own gpt-oss. Using only off-the-shelf tools freely available online and simple API calls, researchers produced non-consensual explicit images in about 13% of attempts on DALL·E 3, under 10% on Nano Banana 2 and about 3% on GPT-Image-2, without needing any custom or frontier model. The research will be presented at NeurIPS in November, at a time when regulators in the US, Australia and the UK are doing little to meaningfully curb the spread of this kind of content.

First harmonised standard supporting the EU AI Act published in the Official Journal

The European Commission published the reference to the first harmonised standard supporting the AI Act in the EU's Official Journal on October 19, EN 18286:2026, covering a quality management system built specifically for the regulation's requirements. The standard, developed by European standardisation bodies CEN and CENELEC, translates the Article 17 quality management obligations for providers of high-risk AI systems into concrete processes organised around an AI system's lifecycle, and is designed to sit on top of existing standards like ISO/IEC 42001 or ISO 9001 rather than replace them. Its formal publication through Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/2274 gives companies that implement it a presumption of conformity with the corresponding requirements, something no other AI quality management standard has offered until now. For a business planning to place a high-risk AI system on the EU market, having a specific, legally recognized compliance pathway means less uncertainty about how to demonstrate adherence to the regulation, particularly for those not yet certified under ISO/IEC 42001.

MITRE ATT&CK v20 formally adds AI abuse to its threat-tracking framework

MITRE released version 20 of its ATT&CK framework, the most widely used tool for cataloguing attacker tactics worldwide, adding nine new Enterprise techniques that explicitly cover AI abuse. Prompt injection is now split into two separate techniques, one for malicious instructions an attacker types directly into an LLM and another for instructions hidden in content the model simply reads, while other additions cover attackers abusing an organization's own AI agents to steal data and poisoning web content to skew AI search and assistant recommendations. The release adds 15 new detection strategies overall, six for Enterprise and nine for industrial control systems (ICS), backed by 38 analytics, and documents an AI-assisted breach of Mexican government institutions that reached a municipal water utility. MITRE says the biggest change in this release is its 'expanded coverage of recent adversary activity and tradecraft,' including how attackers bypass MFA and CAPTCHA checks. For a business building its own threat-detection program, folding AI abuse into such a widely used reference framework means related controls will start showing up soon in off-the-shelf security tools it already uses.

For a business building its own support chatbot, the case shows that the instructions behind an AI assistant, the text defining what it will and won't say, function as a compliance document rather than a mere technical detail, since anyone who views the page's source code can read them.
